Published March 2021.

**Who he is.** Brian Raffel founded Raven Software in 1990 with his brother Steve
and was still heading the studio when this interview was conducted — which makes
him about as close to a primary source on Heretic, Hexen and Hexen II as it is
possible to get.

Inky is upfront about the constraints: Raffel's schedule and Raven's PR approval
process meant the published interview is a substantially trimmed version of a much
longer question list he had originally drafted. He notes as compensation that
Raffel brought in a second voice for the bonus section.

## Ground the conversation covers

**Before the company.** Raffel's background — Madison, Wisconsin, a large family,
and a first career as a high-school art teacher — and how a Dungeons & Dragons
module he and Steve worked on in the summers turned into a games company. The
brothers taught themselves computer art on Amigas in the late 1980s with no
internet and no schools to learn from, and the module became their first game,
*Black Crypt*. There is a good aside on where the studio's name came from: a
character belonging to a friend from their D&D group, chosen because Steve
insisted on something medieval rather than something that sounded like a computer
firm. The page includes a picture of Raven's first logo attempt.

**How the id Software partnership started.** A genuinely good story that turns on
coincidence: needing a PC programmer after a publisher pushed them off the Amiga,
the Raffels advertised in a Madison newspaper, not knowing that id — then working
on Wolfenstein and Commander Keen — was about a mile away. John Romero saw the ad
and made contact. Raffel is unequivocal that Raven would not have survived without
the relationship.

**What he actually did.** He unpicks his own credits, which is useful, because the
titles do not describe the reality of a tiny 1990s studio. Steve was president; he
handled payroll, legal and accounting alongside art and project direction; Romero
was executive producer. He and Steve, with Brian Pelletier and later Shane Gurno,
made the weapons, creatures and textures between them.

**Why Hexen II looks the way it does.** The most quoted-in-passing answer among
mappers: the four horsemen of the apocalypse plus a time-travel conceit drove the
choice of settings, and with a small team on new Quake engine technology, the team
scanned photographs of wall art from those ancient civilisations — cutting-edge
then — and derived original textures from those styles.

**Why the series is not more of an RPG.** Raffel explains that Heretic was
originally designed as something considerably more complicated, and that id, fresh
from Doom, pushed to simplify it along Doom's lines with a fantasy treatment — a
decision he defends as correct for the moment.

**His own maps.** He cannot recall most of them, particularly the deathmatch ones,
but names the one he is proudest of: the opening level of Hexen, with the bell
tower, the breaking stained glass and the blowing leaves.

The page carries photographs of Raffel at Raven's first office in 1990 and with
Steve at the studio's twenty-fifth anniversary in 2015.

## The bonus section

Appended to the interview is a separate written piece by **Michael Raymond-Judy**,
a designer on many early Raven games and the author of most of the Heretic story,
on how the series' lore was written and how it changed.

It is the more interesting half for anyone who cares about continuity, because it
is candid about how ad hoc the process was: the Heretic story was not finished
until near the end of development and was assembled backwards from what the three
hubs already contained; the "Serpent Riders" concept generalised outward from
D'Sparil, with a private "world serpent" reading that never made it into the game;
Tolkien and Michael Moorcock supplied the mental image of the world; and the
Heretic expansion's plot was kept thin because the schedule demanded it.

He also walks through the naming, which is the part most likely to be new to
readers — the corrupted leaders of the Church, the Legion and the Arcanum were
named for what they had become rather than what they were, with one name meaning
traitor and another built from Tolkien's Elvish — and where the player-character
names came from, largely his own D&D campaigns and novels. He is wry about how
seriously fans came to take text he wrote as manual copy, and notes he wrote more
carefully for Hexen as a result.
