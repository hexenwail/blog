Published November 2020. The longest of the three interviews, and the most
technically substantial.

**Who he is.** Kevin Schilder wrote the music and designed the sound for the
Heretic and Hexen games — the opening theme everyone recognises, the ettin noises,
the voice lines players still quote. He came to Raven through the Raffel brothers,
whom he met at college and gamed with; a saxophonist from the age of nine with
degrees in performance and music education, he moved into games as the studio
formed around him.

Inky flags that Schilder has spoken at length elsewhere, particularly on Scott
Tobin's *[Composers Play](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCwyTjJvvJU0GUFk_17XiIOg)*
series, and marks the questions that deliberately approach previously-covered
ground from a different angle so as not to repeat it.

## Ground the conversation covers

**How the D&D group became a studio.** His account of the same origin story Brian
Raffel tells, from the other side of the table — the Friday night campaigns, the
early D&D-like computer games that got the group's attention, and *Dungeon Master*
as the specific game that made the Raffels think they could build one themselves.
He describes arriving at Raven while Heretic was in progress and recognising it as
the D&D fantasy world they had all wanted, finally rendered with real motion.

**The Hexen II audio credit, explained.** The most valuable factual correction in
the interview for Hexen II players, and it is not widely known. Schilder was
simultaneously doing all music and sound for *Take No Prisoners* — where he was
also creative director — and *Mageslayer*. Stretched past capacity, he hired
Raven's first additional sound designer, **Chia Chin Lee**, who became the primary
audio lead on Hexen II and made its sound effects. Schilder continued to write
Hexen II's music but is candid that this is the game in the series he feels least
connected to, and generous about Lee's work and his own decision to give him room.

**Composition method.** He describes working from very small fragments — a phrase
or a rhythm caught from a random CD, a film score, or improvisation at the piano —
and then filling in what he could hear was missing, part by part. He had almost no
direction: as the only musician and sound designer at the studio he set format,
style, instrumentation and structure himself, taking general reactions from
colleagues but with no one to collaborate with on the actual work.

**Tools and MIDI craft.** He used Cubase for music and Sound Forge for sound
effects, and stayed with them deliberately. The best passage in the interview is on
composing natively in MIDI and trying to make it sound human: he applied his
orchestration training to keep general-MIDI parts inside the real instruments'
playable ranges — going outside them, he says, is what makes other people's MIDI
sound wrong — and allowed for players needing to breathe and making small
imperfections.

**How Hexen II's music relates to Hexen's.** With little time available, he
deliberately reprised material from Hexen to tie the series together, and makes an
unembarrassed case for it by analogy with film scoring. Where he did write new
material, the Roman and Egyptian themes drove the choices, including an oboe to
signal Egypt — decided by ear rather than by research.

**Maps and music, and when each was written.** On why Heretic's music could not be
written to finished maps (nobody could wait for anybody; the games were made in a
year to eighteen months) and how Hexen's more deliberate design let him work from
written descriptions of each map's mood and location before it existed. He also
explains the introduction of random ambient sound in Hexen, added to make maps feel
inhabited, and constrained hard by file size and load time.

**Ogg, and one oddity.** He covers the shift from Red Book audio to compressed
formats in Hexen II and why Ogg was preferred to MP3. Asked about an unusual
*Portal of Praevus* track that no regular map in that mission pack plays, he says
plainly that it is not his, guesses it was Chia Chin Lee's late-project joke, and
suggests going and asking him.

**Voices and Foley.** Confirmation that he performed the reversed Disciple speech
in Heretic and Hexen himself, with a good explanation of the craft involved —
choosing phrases that would sound good backwards, and performing them with
inflections that sound absurd forwards. He is clear that the voice on the Hexen hub
walls was **Tim Moore**, a Raven designer, not him. And the ice spell sounds in
Hexen were Foley he made with ice cube trays from his freezer.

**Why the series stopped.** His read on the franchise's disappearance: once Raven
was an Activision studio there was no corporate appetite, despite people inside
wanting a remake; *Heretic II* underperforming was the practical reason there were
no sequels; and he has found it hard even to trace who has held the rights since.

**Afterwards.** Later career highlights including *Jedi Knight II: Jedi Outcast*
with Steve Raffel and re-creating the *Star Trek: Voyager* theme, his move out of
composition into management as projects grew, and his layoff in Raven's major
downsizing. He is frank about the Human Head split in 1997 and the layoffs a decade
later having broken up the original team, and warm about hearing from players
decades on. His credits are on
[kevinschilder.com](http://www.kevinschilder.com/).
