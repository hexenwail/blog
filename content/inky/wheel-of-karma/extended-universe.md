The most unusual page on Inky's site, and the most useful to other mappers: rather
than keeping his setting to himself, he publishes design guidance for three
countries years ahead of the maps that will use them, so anyone else can build
there too.

## The in-fiction argument

The framing device is neat. Thyrion is known to players through the *Chronicles of
Deeds*, written by scholars — and scholars, he points out, have biases, blind
spots and gaps. Taking their four great powers (Blackmarsh, Mazaera, Thysis,
Septimus) as the whole world is a mistake of the source, not a fact about the
setting. Tulku did not appear from nowhere after Eidolon's defeat; it was simply
outside the chroniclers' field of view. That reasoning opens room for other
civilisations without contradicting anything canonical.

Three of them will appear in Wheel Of Karma's third instalment, which he did not
expect to release before the mid-2020s. The guidance below is explicitly
non-binding — inspiration, not a specification — and he offers support and
entities to anyone who wants to use it. One shared visual motif he suggests as
connective tissue is Quake's sundagger pattern, which Wheel Of Karma uses often.

## Agawara

Based on medieval Japan, with the Katsura Imperial Villa as the specific reference.

The look is light and delicate: wooden strips, beams and posts against white paper
walls, producing near-monochrome rooms full of horizontals, verticals and grids,
where occasional colour becomes a landmark. His suggested vocabulary includes
calligraphy panels, tatami, red paper lanterns, zen rock gardens, stone lanterns,
stepping stones, ponds, bamboo, and shoji-like transoms above doors.

The gameplay consequences are the interesting part, because the architecture
dictates them. Walls are thin, made of wood and paper or fabric, likely to be
breakable, and likely to slide — so **rotating doors do not belong here**;
doors slide and open on approach. Buildings are low and offer little verticality,
spreading instead as a maze of near-identical rooms. Sight lines indoors are
short, windows are few and translucent rather than see-through, and a wall that
slides or breaks can reveal a threat with no warning. He suggests players will
watch eye level and miss what is on the beams above them. Courtyard gardens
provide the outdoor space but deliberately do not help you build a mental map.
Narrow interiors favour melee monsters, and reducing mana pickups pushes players
onto their first weapon. The region has an endemic monster: helmeted "samurai
golems".

## Qazzarith

Based on the Persian and Arabic world, with his own implementation leaning
specifically Uzbek.

An arid land in hot desert. The architecture works on a hard contrast between
outside and inside: massive thick brown stone walls that keep out heat, sandstorms
and curiosity, concealing far more elaborate interiors with columns, mosaics,
covered galleries and courtyards. Water is wealth, so private ponds signal status.
The people are scholars, known for libraries, mathematics and astronomy, with
refined but strictly non-representational decorative art, and they sit on carpets
rather than chairs. Blue — the colour of water and clear sky — dominates the
prestige work, especially mosaics and cupolas. He offers the Registan in Samarkand
as the reference image.

For textures he suggests reusing the vanilla Thysis Egyptian set, plus Heretic II
material, WhirledTsar's working pack from their 2022 map *Qazzarith Library*, and
Simon "Sock" O'Callaghan's Quake map *[One Thousand
Cuts](https://web.archive.org/web/20201012221640/http://www.simonoc.com/pages/design/sp/1000cuts.htm)*.
Downloadable packs for each are on his page; we do not mirror them.

Layout-wise, cities are fortresses — against the desert and against raiders — so
exteriors are courtyards and walled plazas rather than landscape, and siege
engines such as ballistas and catapults fit naturally. Monsters resemble those of
Thysis, given the similar ecosystem, with Shadow Wizards as the signature threat.
There is a horror seam too: scholars who pursued knowledge too far and returned as
the brotherhood players know as skull wizards, with appropriately disturbing
imagery in their deepest sanctuaries — for which he points at Kell's KDM texture
set.

## Babalpor

Based on Mesopotamia, and Babylon in particular.

Also desert, and sharing Thysis's heavy brown palette, but irrigated by large
rivers — so the relieving colour is green rather than blue, with oases and hanging
gardens. Mosaics again, plus bas-reliefs, and here the imagery *is*
representational: animals, plants, soldiers. The people are pragmatic rather than
intellectual, warriors and farmers, and skilled builders whose plain massive
architecture is enlivened by mechanisms and moving parts, crenellations, friezes
and round arches. The Ishtar Gate is his reference.

For textures, the vanilla Thysis set again, plus a Babylonian pack sourced by
WhirledTsar from *Indiana Jones & The Infernal Machine*.

Cities are fortified like Qazzarith's but feel more like a southern Blackmarsh —
ordinary streets, bazaars, shops — with luxury expressed through gardens, water
features and terracing rather than towers and domes, ziggurats excepted. Expect
largely open-air maps. Their engineering shows up as transport: teleporters are
rare, but elevators, moving platforms, wind tunnels and low-gravity fields are
common, which supports fast vertical movement — useful, since flying imps are a
regular nuisance. Encounters skew toward packs and swarms in the plazas, with
isolated archers sniping from behind crenellations at long range, so standing
still is not an option.
